How to film your BJJ rolls

To film your BJJ rolls, you only need a phone and a small tripod or clamp. Put the camera at the side of the mat, a little above head height, far enough back to keep both of you in frame for the whole round. Ask your partner before you press record, film full rounds rather than highlights, and watch the footage within a day or two, while you still remember how the round felt.

By the Matsense team, built and tested by IBJJF world champions

Why film your rolls

After a hard round, your memory keeps one or two moments and fills in the rest. The footage shows what you actually do, not what you think you do.

  • You see the almosts. The sweep that almost went through and the pass you almost stopped. They are easy to miss from inside the round.
  • You see what came before. The problem usually starts a grip or a position before the moment you remember.
  • You see your progress. Footage from three months ago next to footage from today shows changes you cannot feel from day to day.

What you need

  • A phone. Any recent phone camera is enough. Film in landscape, at normal speed.
  • A tripod, clamp or wall mount. A cheap phone tripod works. A clamp on a wall bar or a fence is useful when there is no floor space.
  • Do not disturb mode. So a call or a notification does not stop the recording in the middle of a round.
  • Storage and battery. A full class of rounds takes a lot of space. Clear the phone before training, and bring a power bank.

Where to put the camera

  • At the side of the mat, a step back. Far enough that you do not roll out of frame when the round moves.
  • A little above head height, angled down. From a higher angle, one body blocks the other less often, and you can see grips and hips.
  • Not facing a window or a bright light. Backlight turns both athletes into dark shapes.
  • Away from the busy area. Pick a corner where other pairs are less likely to roll in front of the camera.

Check the frame before the round starts. If you can see both athletes from head to feet, it is good enough.

Ask first: filming etiquette

  • Ask your coach whether the academy allows filming, and if there are rules about it.
  • Ask your partner before each round. Some people do not want to be filmed, and that is their call.
  • Do not post someone else without asking. Footage for your own study is one thing. Posting it publicly is another, and needs your partner's permission.
  • Roll the same way you always do. The camera is for learning, not for winning the round. Going harder because you are filmed ruins both the round and the footage.

What to film

  • Full rounds, not highlights. The moments that matter are often in the quiet parts of the round.
  • Rounds with partners at your level and above. They show your real problems faster.
  • Positional rounds. When you are working on one position, film rounds that start from it.
  • Your competition matches. Ask a teammate to film from as close to the mat as the event allows. Many large tournaments are also streamed, and you can often find your match in the replay.

What to do with the footage

  1. Watch it within a day or two. While you still remember what you were trying to do.
  2. Look for the almosts and the moments that decided the round. Read how in How to analyze a BJJ match.
  3. Pick one thing to work on. One task you will drill this week, not a list of ten.
  4. Keep the footage by date. So you can come back to it in a few months and compare.

Questions people ask

Should I film my BJJ rolls?

Yes, if your academy and your partners are fine with it. Footage shows what you actually do in a round, not what you remember, and it is the clearest way to see your mistakes and your progress over time.

Is it okay to film rolls at my gym?

Ask your coach first, and ask your partner before each round. Keep the footage for your own study, and do not post anyone publicly without their permission.

What is the best camera angle for filming BJJ?

From the side of the mat, a step back, a little above head height and angled down. That keeps both athletes in frame and shows grips and hips with less blocking.

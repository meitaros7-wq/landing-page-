What to do after losing a BJJ match

Losing a BJJ match hurts, and every competitor goes through it. Give yourself a day, then rewatch the match. Find the moment it was decided and the almosts that could have changed it, turn them into one to three training tasks, and check them at your next competition. A loss on video tells you more about your game than weeks of training.

By the Matsense team, built and tested by IBJJF world champions

First, give it a day

Feeling bad after a loss is normal, and it says nothing about your future in jiu-jitsu. Do not make big decisions on the day of the tournament. Talk to your coach and your teammates, rest, and come back to the match when you can look at it calmly.

Get the footage

Ask the teammate who filmed you for the video, or look for your match in the event's stream replay if it was streamed. If there is no video at all, write down everything you remember on the same day, in order, before the details fade.

Rewatch it, even if it hurts

Your memory of a lost match is usually the last bad moment. The footage almost always shows that the loss started earlier.

  1. Watch it once from start to finish, without stopping, to see the whole story.
  2. Find the moment the match was decided. The pass, the sweep, the score or the submission.
  3. Go back a few seconds before it. What grip, position or decision led there?
  4. Go through the almosts, yours and your opponent's: the moments where the match nearly went another way.

Why did you lose? Common patterns

  • You lost the start. You were taken down, or pulled into a guard you did not want. Train the first exchange: your grips, your takedown or your guard pull, and your answer to theirs.
  • You got passed. Look at where your guard frames broke. It is usually a grip or a distance problem a step before the pass.
  • You could not finish. The submission or the sweep was there and slipped away. Find what was missing: a grip, an angle, the timing or the step before.
  • You lost on points or advantages. Check what you did with the score in the last minute, and whether you knew the rules of the event. See IBJJF points explained.
  • You ran out of gas. Look at when your pace dropped. Adrenaline in a first tournament burns energy fast, and controlled breathing and pacing can be trained.
  • You were stuck in one position. If you spent most of the match in the same place, that position is your next project.

Find what went right too

Every match, also a lost one, has moments where your game worked. Note them. They are part of your strong areas, and your next game plan is built on them.

Turn the loss into a plan

  1. Pick one to three tasks. Short and specific, for example "keep the knee shield frame when they switch to the other side", not "improve my guard".
  2. Drill each one from the exact position. Start where the problem happened.
  3. Test it in positional sparring, against resistance, and then in full rounds.
  4. Check it at your next competition. Did the same moment come up, and did it go your way this time?

If it was your first tournament

Many competitors lose at their first tournament, and many of them say it was the match that taught them the most. You now have something training cannot give you: a real match against an opponent you did not know, under pressure, on video. Use it, and sign up for the next one.

Questions people ask

Should I rewatch a BJJ match I lost?

Yes, after a day or two, when you can look at it calmly. The footage shows where the loss really started, which is usually earlier than the moment you remember.

What should I work on after losing a BJJ tournament?

Find the moment the match was decided and the almosts that could have changed it, then turn them into one to three specific tasks. Drill them from the exact position, test them in positional sparring, and check them at your next competition.

Is it normal to lose your first BJJ tournament?

Yes. Many competitors lose at their first tournament. The match is still valuable: it shows how your game works under real pressure, against someone you do not know.
